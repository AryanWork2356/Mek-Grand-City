Infrastructure

How Atal Setu and Metro Line 5 are reshaping Thane's property prices

Two infrastructure projects are landing on the same corridor within years of each other — here's what each one actually changes for a buyer in Mumbra and Thane, and why timing matters.

Atal Setu: already live, already cutting commute times

The Mumbai Trans Harbour Link (Atal Setu) is India's longest sea bridge, connecting Mumbai to Navi Mumbai. Since opening, it has cut the travel time between Sewri and Navi Mumbai from roughly 90 minutes down to about 20 minutes, and it unlocks a direct route toward JNPT, the Navi Mumbai International Airport, and South Mumbai. For anyone evaluating a home in the Thane–Mumbra belt, this is the single biggest reason the "far from the city" argument against this corridor no longer holds the way it did a few years ago.

Metro Line 5: the corridor's second catalyst

Metro Line 5 runs from Thane through Bhiwandi to Kalyan, covering roughly 25 km across 17 stations, and is expected to carry around 10 lakh riders a day once fully operational. Phase 1 — Kapurbawdi to Dhamankar Naka — has reportedly crossed 99% of its civil works and is targeted for a December 2026 commercial launch, with Metro Line 5 pushing rapid transit deep into what is already one of the Mumbai Metropolitan Region's largest affordable and mid-budget housing markets.

What this means for prices

Corridors that gain metro access ahead of most of the region typically see pricing move in two stages: a first jump when the project is confirmed and under visible construction, and a second when it actually opens and commute times become real rather than projected. With Phase 1 of Line 5 now in its final stretch, Mumbra and the wider Thane–Bhiwandi–Kalyan belt is arguably past the first stage and approaching the second — which is usually the window where early buyers see the most benefit relative to what they paid.

What to check before you buy on an "upcoming infrastructure" story

  • Confirm the project's actual completion percentage from recent news, not just the original announcement — infrastructure timelines in India commonly slip, and "upcoming" can mean two very different things depending on how far along it is.
  • Check the real distance from the specific project to the nearest proposed station — "near Metro Line 5" can mean 500 metres or 5 kilometres.
  • Separate the infrastructure story from the property's own fundamentals (RERA registration, builder track record, construction stage) — a good location doesn't fix a badly executed project.

Legal & Safety

What RERA registration actually protects you from

"RERA registered" gets printed on every project brochure in India — here's what the law actually requires, and the five things it lets you check before you pay a rupee.

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 — RERA — was introduced to regulate India's real estate sector and protect homebuyers after years of project delays, unclear pricing, and limited recourse when things went wrong. Any residential or commercial project above a certain size must register with the state's RERA authority before it can be advertised, marketed, or sold at all. In Maharashtra, that's MahaRERA.

What RERA registration actually guarantees

  • Mandatory disclosure. The developer must publish project details, approvals, land title status, and timelines on the public RERA portal — not just what's convenient to put in a brochure.
  • Standardised area calculation. RERA fixed the definition of carpet area across the country, closing a loophole where builders used to quote inflated "super area" figures to make pricing look better than it was.
  • Delay penalties. If a developer misses the promised possession date, RERA entitles the buyer to compensation or a refund with interest — this is a major reason project delivery timelines have tightened industry-wide since the Act came in.
  • Five-year structural liability. Builders remain responsible for structural defects for five years after possession; defects found in that window must be fixed at no cost within 30 days.
  • A legal grievance channel. Disputes go to a dedicated RERA tribunal rather than a slow civil court process, which is usually faster for buyers.

How to actually verify it — not just take the brochure's word

Every state RERA authority runs a public portal. In Maharashtra, that's maharera.maharashtra.gov.in. Search the project's RERA registration number directly on the portal — this confirms the registration is real, shows the promoter's full disclosure, the sanctioned plan, and the current project status, all before you pay a booking amount.

Buyer Basics

Carpet area vs built-up area vs super built-up area, explained simply

Three numbers, three different meanings, and the gap between them is exactly where a lot of buyer confusion (and occasional overpricing) comes from.

Carpet area

The actual usable floor space inside your unit's walls — literally the area you could cover with a carpet. It excludes the walls themselves, balconies, and any shafts. Since RERA, this is the legally standardised figure every registered project must disclose, and it's the single most honest number for comparing two different projects.

Built-up area

Carpet area plus the thickness of your unit's own walls, plus any balcony or terrace attached to the unit. This is typically 10–20% larger than the carpet area. It still doesn't include anything shared with other residents.

Super built-up area (the "saleable area")

Built-up area plus a proportional share of common areas — lobbies, staircases, lifts, corridors, clubhouse, and other shared amenities. This is usually 25–30% larger than the built-up area, and it's the number most developers actually price against. The proportion added for shared space is called the loading factor, and it commonly ranges from 25% to as high as 60% depending on the project.

Why this matters when comparing two projects

If one project quotes a price per carpet sq ft and another quotes per super built-up sq ft, they are not comparable numbers at all — the second project could look cheaper per square foot while actually costing more for the same usable space. Always ask which area basis a quoted rate is calculated on, and convert everything to the same basis before comparing.

Finance

Home loan tax benefits in India: what you can actually claim

Two sections of the Income Tax Act do the heavy lifting for a home loan — here's what each one covers, and the one choice that determines whether you can claim either at all.

Section 24(b) — interest deduction

On a self-occupied property under the old tax regime, you can claim up to ₹2 lakh a year in home loan interest as a deduction. This is usually the larger of the two benefits in the early years of a loan, when interest makes up most of your EMI.

Section 80C — principal repayment

The principal portion of your EMI qualifies under Section 80C, up to ₹1.5 lakh a year — though this is a shared cap with other common 80C investments (PF, ELSS, life insurance premiums, etc.), not an amount exclusive to your home loan. Stamp duty and registration charges also qualify under 80C, but only in the year they're actually paid, and still within that same combined ₹1.5 lakh ceiling.

Put together

Between the two sections, a self-occupied homeowner can reduce taxable income by up to ₹3.5 lakh a year — ₹2 lakh under Section 24(b) and ₹1.5 lakh under 80C. On a joint home loan, where both co-owners are also co-borrowers, each can claim these limits individually — potentially doubling the combined benefit to ₹7 lakh across two incomes.

The catch: old regime vs. new regime

Since 2023–24, the new tax regime is the default option when filing returns in India, and it does not allow these home loan deductions for a self-occupied property. If you want to claim Section 24(b) or 80C benefits on a home loan, you need to actively opt into the old tax regime at filing time — it's worth running the comparison with an accountant before assuming either regime is automatically better for your situation.

This is general information, not tax advice — confirm current limits and your own eligibility with a chartered accountant before filing, since allowances can change with each year's budget.
First-Time Buyers

The first-time homebuyer checklist: what to verify before you book

Buying your first home is mostly about asking the right questions in the right order. Here's the sequence that catches the most common regrets.

1. Check the RERA registration first, before anything else

Before discussing price, floor plans, or possession dates, look the project up on your state's RERA portal. This single step filters out unregistered or misrepresented projects before you invest any more time.

2. Get your CIBIL score in order 2–3 months early

Lenders check your credit score before approving a home loan, and errors or old overdue accounts can quietly drag your score down. Pull your report and resolve any issues at least two to three months before you plan to apply — fixing a credit report takes longer than most buyers expect.

3. Budget beyond just the sale price

Factor in stamp duty (typically 5–8% of the property's value depending on the state), registration charges, and keep a contingency buffer of 2–3% for costs that come up during the process. A down payment calculated only against the base price regularly leaves buyers short at registration.

4. Confirm which area figure the price is based on

As covered above, always confirm whether a quoted rate is per carpet area, built-up area, or super built-up area before comparing two projects — see our area-measurement guide for the full breakdown.

5. Think past the next 2–3 years

A first home is rarely a lifetime decision — but configuration (2 BHK vs. 3 BHK), resale potential, and how the surrounding corridor is likely to develop over the next decade are all worth weighing against your near-term budget, not just today's immediate need.

6. Visit the actual site, not just the sample flat

A sample flat shows you finishes; the actual site shows you construction quality, how far along the project really is, and the real surrounding neighbourhood — all things a brochure can't fully convey.
